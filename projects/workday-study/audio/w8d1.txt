Week 8, day 1. Security policies. [[slnc 1200]] Today's objective is Domain versus business process Security Policies, in the Security domain. [[slnc 600]] In one line: Separate secured-item access from business-process participation, then activate changes. [[slnc 1200]] Let's start with the mental model. [[slnc 500]] Domain policies grant View or Modify (and Get or Put for integrations) to items in a domain; Modify includes View, Put includes Get, and child domains inherit from their parent. Each business process type has one business process policy, shared by all of its definitions, that controls who can initiate, act, approve, delegate, cancel, rescind, deny, and more. Subprocesses do not inherit the parent's policy. Policy changes stay pending until you run Activate Pending Security Policy Changes; Activate Previous Security Timestamp reverts. Changes to security group membership take effect immediately. Template-driven processes such as Termination and Change Job need both the business process policy and the domain policies for each section of the page. [[slnc 1200]] Now, the distinctions you need to know. [[slnc 500]] Can see or modify an item?: Domain policy. [[slnc 700]] Can initiate or approve an event?: business process policy. [[slnc 700]] Template-driven process: Needs both business process and domain permissions. [[slnc 700]]  [[slnc 600]] Here's how it shows up at work. [[slnc 500]] If HR can view worker data but cannot initiate Change Job, inspect Change Job business process security rather than adding broad domain access. [[slnc 1200]] Task and report names to recognize: Edit Domain Security Policy, Edit Business Process Security Policy, Activate Pending Security Policy Changes, and View Security for Securable Item. [[slnc 1200]] And the exam trap to watch for. [[slnc 500]] Saving a policy edit does not activate it. Review, activate, and test with suitable accounts. [[slnc 1500]] Quick check.
